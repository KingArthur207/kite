# Kite: sourced facts

Checked Sat Oct 24 2026, early morning. Each entry gives the number, where it comes from, the date of the source and how much to trust it. Links were opened unless the entry says otherwise.

## Short version

- The "$50,000 guide dog" holds up only as one school's figure. Guiding Eyes for the Blind says $50,000 to raise and train a dog. Other US schools say $25,000 (Freedom Guide Dogs) to $75,000 (The Seeing Eye). The blind person pays nothing or close to it; donors pay. Say "Guiding Eyes puts the cost of one guide dog at $50,000", never "a guide dog costs a blind person $50,000".
- The "2%" is a 30-year-old number. The only traceable source is a 1995 note in the Journal of Visual Impairment & Blindness, using data from around 1990. Guide Dogs for the Blind and the American Foundation for the Blind said in 2022 that they could not find a current prevalence estimate. Drop it. A sourced replacement: fewer than 10,000 working guide dogs in the US, against about 1 million Americans who are blind.

Safest numbers for the slide:

1. "Guiding Eyes for the Blind puts the cost of raising and training one guide dog at $50,000." Attributed, on the school's own site, and the school stands behind it.
2. "About 1 million Americans are blind (JAMA Ophthalmology, 2021)." Peer reviewed, built on CDC data, easy to defend.

If there's room for a third: "fewer than 10,000 guide dogs work in the US" (IGDF 2018 figure, reported by Nippon.com). The source is secondary and seven years old, so say "fewer than 10,000" and don't use a precise number.

What this means for the pitch: $50,000 is defensible with attribution. The cost falls on donors, and the GDB/AFB survey (below) found that 22% of non-users said they couldn't afford a guide dog, probably because of food and vet bills rather than the dog itself. If Kite is pitched on cost, compare it to what the school spends, and don't imply blind people are paying it.

## How many blind people use a guide dog (the "2%")

Where it comes from. Manduchi and Kurniawan (2011) write that "only 2 percent used a dog guide", citing "Demographics Update: Alternate estimate of the number of guide dog users", Journal of Visual Impairment & Blindness 89(2), 1995, and they describe the cane figures beside it as 1990 data ([paper, page 3](https://web.archive.org/web/2020id_/https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/~manduchi/papers/MobilityAccidents.pdf)). I couldn't open the 1995 note itself. Other places that repeat 2% (a [Guiding Eyes appeal](https://give.guidingeyes.org/Vaughngivesback), [Easterseals Tech](https://eastersealstech.com/tag/lumen-glasses), stats aggregators) cite nothing. One Perkins page uses "fewer than 2 percent" for white cane users instead ([Perkins](https://www.perkins.org/10-little-known-facts-about-blindness/)), so the number gets mixed up in circulation as well.

Nobody has a current figure. In the GDB and AFB study, a researcher said "it was hard even to identify a prevalence estimate for the percentage of folks who use guide dogs in the US" ([podcast transcript](https://www.guidedogs.com/uploads/files/Podcast-Transcripts/Central_Bark_A_GDB_Podcast_The_Future_of_Guide_Dogs_in_2022_and_Beyond_MIX-transcript.pdf), Oct 2022). A 2025 NFB journal article (Silverman et al., [Journal of Blindness Innovation and Research 15(1)](https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/jbir/jbir25/jbir150102.html)) also reports a lack of current data. That page blocked automated reads, so this rests on its abstract and search summaries.

What we can say instead:

| Figure | Source | Date | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| US: "less than 10,000" active guide dogs | IGDF 2018 annual report, as reported by [Nippon.com](https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-data/h00644/japan-struggling-to-increase-the-number-of-active-guide-dogs.html) | Feb 27, 2020 | Medium. Secondary, and counts dogs, not users. |
| Worldwide: 18,978 guide dogs working; 2,600 trained in 2025; 100 member schools in 34 countries | [IGDF facts and figures](https://www.igdf.org.uk/about-us/facts-and-figures/) | As of Dec 31, 2025 | High. |
| Ratio: under 10,000 dogs to about 1.08 million blind Americans is under 1% | Derived by us from the two rows above and Flaxman et al. below | 2017 to 2018 data | Low. A back-of-envelope ratio across different definitions. Don't put it on a slide. |

The 2022 GDB/AFB survey of 533 blind and low-vision adults ([AFB summary](https://afb.org/research-and-initiatives/research/summaries/role-guide-dogs), [full report PDF](https://guidedogs.com/uploads/files/Resources/GDB_AFB_Role_Of_Guide_Dogs_Report_Accessible_FINAL.pdf)) found that 53% of respondents used a guide dog. That reflects who was recruited and is not a national rate. Its findings on non-users are more useful to us. Of 236 non-users, 31% said they didn't want the responsibility of caring for a dog and 22% said they couldn't afford one. Others said they weren't active enough or didn't feel they needed one. So many people don't want a dog at all, and the wait is only one of the reasons. That is an argument for Kite that doesn't depend on the 2%.

The overhead point is the strongest one: the cane can't find a branch or sign above waist height, and the Access Board rule exists because of that. Kite's drone and chest camera can see head-height obstacles, so this is where the pitch can make a concrete claim.

## Injuries and falls

Manduchi and Kurniawan surveyed more than 300 legally blind or blind adults ([Insight: Research and Practice in Visual Impairment and Blindness 4(2), Fall 2011](https://web.archive.org/web/2020id_/https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/~manduchi/papers/MobilityAccidents.pdf)). Read from the paper:

- Among respondents who are blind, only 2% had never had a head-level accident (12% among the legally blind). Head-level means hitting something at chest height or above.
- 9% of blind and 18% of legally blind respondents had head-level accidents more than once a month.
- 86% of head-level accidents happened outdoors, mostly tree branches, then poles, signs, construction equipment and trucks.
- 23% of head-level accidents had medical consequences, such as stitches or broken teeth.
- Falls: 5% of blind and 10% of legally blind respondents fell more than once a month, and 36% said falls had medical consequences.
- Accident frequency was about the same for cane users and guide dog users.

Confidence: medium. It's an online survey of independent travelers from 2010, not a representative sample, as the authors say themselves. The figures are fine for the Devpost writeup and risky on a slide. I didn't find a sourced US number for blind pedestrians hit by vehicles. NHTSA crash data doesn't record whether a pedestrian was blind.

Say "a guide dog works for roughly six to ten years", which means a handler goes through several dogs and several waits over a lifetime. The GDB/AFB report notes that 45% of the guide dog users it surveyed had used guide dogs for 20 years or more.
